Species guides

General public information to help you know what you're looking at. This page does not identify your specimen — for that, submit a photo (demo). Sources: CDC, Michigan DHHS, and university extension programs — full list at the bottom.

This is not medical advice. Species info below is educational only and never a diagnosis. If you were bitten, see a clinician — don't try to ID risk yourself. CDC tick info · Michigan DHHS

🕷️ Blacklegged tick Ixodes scapularis · "deer tick"

  • Looks like: small — nymphs are poppy-seed sized. Adults have dark legs and a reddish body (females) with a dark shield.
  • Where: woods, leaf litter, tall grass. Expanding fast across Michigan — Lyme cases went 553 (2022) → 2,167 (2025) per MDHHS.
  • Why it matters: the main carrier of Lyme disease in the Midwest. Nymphs are the hardest to spot and the most likely to bite unnoticed.
  • Our AI's weak spot: nymphs vs. specks of dirt. Clear, close photos help most.

🕷️ Lone star tick Amblyomma americanum

  • Looks like: females carry a single white dot on the back — the easiest field mark of any tick here. Males have pale edge markings.
  • Where: historically southern, pushing north — now turning up in southern Michigan counties.
  • Why it matters: aggressive biter; associated with ehrlichiosis and alpha-gal (red-meat allergy) — discuss any bite with a clinician.
  • Our AI's weak spot: confused with dog ticks ~8% of the time (see the confusion matrix). We're actively collecting more lone-star photos.

🕷️ American dog tick Dermacentor variabilis

  • Looks like: larger than the blacklegged tick, with white/cream marbling on the back. The tick most people picture.
  • Where: grassy fields, trails, backyards — very common across Michigan.
  • Why it matters: can carry Rocky Mountain spotted fever (rare in Michigan) and tularemia. Common, but lower Lyme risk than blacklegged ticks.
  • Our AI's weak spot: the lone-star confusion runs both ways — see above.

🕷️ Brown dog tick Rhipicephalus sanguineus · "kennel tick"

  • Looks like: uniformly reddish-brown — scutum matches the body color and carries no white markings; small ridges (festoons) along the rear edge. Adults run ~2–3 mm unengorged, males flat.
  • Where: the indoor specialist — kennels, dog beds, homes, often tucked into door or window frames; active indoors year-round. Found throughout the U.S. and worldwide; dogs are the primary host at every life stage, though it may bite people or other mammals.
  • Why it matters: a Rocky Mountain spotted fever reservoir in the southwestern U.S. (rare in Michigan), plus canine babesiosis and canine ehrlichiosis — talk to your vet about tick prevention.
  • Confused with: the American dog tick above. Telling difference: brown dog ticks have no pale marbling — the American dog tick's back shows white/cream markings; brown dog ticks are also the one you'll find living in your house.

🕷️ Gulf Coast tick Amblyomma maculatum · "Gulf Coast ear tick"

  • Looks like: large like the lone star tick, with silvery-white ornamentation. Females show a pale posterior pattern — one median stripe plus two interrupted lateral stripes on a dark scutum; males lack the white plate but carry thin white lines in swirling patterns across the back.
  • Where: open, non-shaded grassland habitat (not deep woods). Historically southeastern, but expanding: CDC maps now show focal populations in midwestern and northeastern states, with established populations as far inland as Illinois and Indiana. Adults quest mid-to-late summer, most responsive during the hottest part of the day.
  • Why it matters: the main vector of Rickettsia parkeri spotted fever; on livestock, adults prefer the ears of cattle. A species to watch in Michigan rather than one you'll commonly meet — yet.
  • Confused with: the lone star tick. Telling difference: lone-star females carry a single solid white dot; Gulf Coast females show a diffuse pale striped pattern instead.

🕷️ Groundhog tick Ixodes cookei · "woodchuck tick"

  • Looks like: brown body with a darker brown scutum; no festoons along the rear edge. Adult females are about sesame-seed size (~3 mm). Closely resembles the blacklegged tick — they are in the same genus, so separating them safely usually needs a microscope.
  • Where: in and near the dens of woodchucks, skunks, and other small mammals — not out in leaf litter like blacklegged ticks. Found across Michigan; occasionally bites people (pets can carry them indoors), with adult activity peaking in summer (July), unlike the spring/fall peaks of blacklegged adults.
  • Why it matters: can carry Powassan virus (a rare but serious tick-borne illness); it is not associated with Lyme disease transmission.
  • Confused with: the blacklegged tick above. Telling difference (for the lab, not your eyes): same genus, near-identical look — a key trait is the absence of festoons, but field ID between the two is unreliable; that's exactly the hard case our model trains on.

🦟 Common mosquitoes Culex spp. · Aedes spp.

  • Culex ("house" mosquitoes): dull brown, bite mostly at dusk/night. The main West Nile virus vector in Michigan — the reason mosquito surveillance exists.
  • Aedes (incl. Asian tiger mosquito): black-and-white banded legs, aggressive daytime biters. Expanding range; watch for them in tires, buckets, and gutters.
  • Photo tip: mosquitoes photograph better than ticks — wing-vein detail survives phone cameras, which is why our Culex recall is the model's best cell (91%, sample).
  • Prevention: dump standing water weekly — the single highest-impact thing a household can do.

🦟 Cattail mosquito Coquillettidia perturbans

  • Looks like: a light-brown mosquito whose legs show an alternating dark-and-light scale pattern. The real giveaway is breeding habitat, not the adult: larvae live in permanent wetlands and pierce the roots of emergent plants — especially cattails — to breathe underwater, so they never need to surface.
  • Where: swamps, bogs, and marshes — EEE's classic Michigan habitat. Females are persistent, painful biters, mostly early evening but willing to bite in the daytime in shady spots. It's the only Coquillettidia species in the U.S. and Canada.
  • Why it matters: EEE virus has been isolated from this species in Michigan, and Michigan ranks among the highest-EEE states in the country (10 human cases, 6 deaths, in 2019 alone per published surveillance). MDHHS flags swamp and bog habitats as the highest-risk zone.
  • Confused with: Culex ("house" mosquitoes) above — both are brown dusk biters. Telling difference: Culex legs are plain brown; cattail mosquitoes show distinct pale banding, and dumping your yard's buckets won't touch them — they breed in wetlands, not containers.

🦟 Asian tiger mosquito Aedes albopictus

  • Looks like: the show-off of the group — jet-black body with bright white markings and a single white stripe running the length of its back, from head down the thorax; legs carry silver-white bands. Small (~5 mm, about ¼ inch), and it waves its banded legs overhead as it bites.
  • Where: artificial containers — tires, buckets, clogged gutters, even bottle caps. Arrived in the U.S. in used-tire shipments in the mid-1980s and spread fast; now established far north of its start. First detected in Michigan in 2017 (Wayne County traps near Livonia), and state surveillance keeps finding it moving north with warmer temperatures.
  • Why it matters: an aggressive daytime biter — unlike the dusk-feeding Culex — and an efficient vector of several mosquito-borne pathogens (dengue, chikungunya, Zika where those circulate). No local Michigan cases from this species, but its presence is why daytime repellent matters.
  • Confused with: Aedes spp. above — the plain "banded-legs, daytime biter" description fits both. Telling difference: only the Asian tiger carries the unbroken white racing stripe down the center of the back.

✅ Prevention checklist

  • Use EPA-registered repellent (DEET, picaridin, or oil of lemon eucalyptus) per label directions.
  • Do a full-body tick check after woods, tall grass, or leaf litter — kids and pets included.
  • Tumble dry clothes on high heat 10 minutes to kill missed ticks.
  • Shower within 2 hours of coming indoors.
  • Keep grass short at the yard edge; clear leaf litter where kids play.
  • Talk to your vet about tick prevention for dogs and cats.

🩺 If you were bitten (information only)

  • Remove the tick with fine-tipped tweezers, grasping close to the skin; pull steadily upward. Clean the area.
  • Note the date and (if you can) save the tick in a sealed bag — a clinician may want to see it.
  • Watch for rash, fever, or flu-like symptoms in the following weeks.
  • See a clinician promptly — especially for blacklegged-tick bites, multiple bites, or any symptoms. Only a clinician can advise on testing or treatment.
